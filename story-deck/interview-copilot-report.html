Persistent interview report · 001

Chiang Mai Lifestyle

The participant is a Chiang Mai resident and event emcee who seeks experiences both for personal enjoyment and to explain local culture and tourism to foreign visitors. Temple visits reliably provide the relaxation, prayer, and photography outcomes they want. By contrast, a Facebook-promoted Japanese festival promised Japanese culture and activities but felt primarily like a food festival with contemporary performances, leading the participant to leave within an hour and regard the visit as wasted time. That mismatch reduced their trust in event promotion and made them more selective, with attendance around once a month considered sufficient. They now reject events using AI-generated promotional images, read social-media feedback, and sometimes ask a trusted friend to attend first and report back privately through LINE. Some friends reportedly share the same uncertainty about whether promoted events will match reality. The central decision implication is that the observed need is not broader event discovery alone, but low-effort, credible evidence that an experience is authentic and matches its promotion before someone commits limited time.

What matters

Top pain points

  1. Event promotion can promise culture, lifestyle, and learning while the actual event primarily offers food or generic entertainment.
  2. The Japanese festival's performances felt contemporary rather than representative of the everyday life or authentic culture the participant expected.
  3. A disappointing event costs scarce personal time; the participant concluded they should not have spent time there.
  4. Promotional claims and images on Facebook are not sufficient for the participant to trust that an event will match its description.
  5. AI-generated promotional images are treated as an immediate signal that the real experience will not resemble the advertisement.
  6. Real photographs still may not provide enough confidence, requiring additional verification from another person.
  7. The participant relies on a friend to assume the risk of attending first, creating a slow and socially costly workaround.
  8. Work limits the participant's ability to try desired experiences such as a homestay.
  9. Without first-hand experiences, the participant lacks the tourist perspective they want for telling useful, credible stories in their work.
  10. The participant can answer some visitors' questions after events but cannot answer everything, and the information appears distributed across websites, Facebook groups, physical places, and personal knowledge.

Opportunity hypothesis

Where the evidence points

A bounded hypothesis is that selective, time-constrained event seekers may value a lightweight credibility layer showing recent, real attendee evidence and a clear comparison between advertised and actual experiences. This could reduce dependence on sending trusted friends first while helping the participant identify culturally substantive events. The interview supports the behavior and workaround, but does not establish demand for a new product or willingness to contribute, trust, or pay.

Inference — validate before treating as fact

Least-effort next step

What to do next

Recruit the participant and four to six similarly selective Chiang Mai residents from their friend network. Show them five real event listings augmented only with recent attendee photos, a short "promised versus experienced" note, and source identity, then compare their attend-or-skip decisions and confidence with the original Facebook listings. Useful validation would be repeated decisions made without contacting a friend, accompanied by participants correctly explaining which evidence changed their confidence; absence of that behavior would weaken the hypothesis.
